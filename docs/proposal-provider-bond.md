# Provider Bond System for the Vellar Bazaar — Design Document

**Status:** design complete, not yet built. All decisions in this document are locked for v1. Implementation begins when this document is approved.

This document designs a bond-and-slash primitive for the Bazaar: sellers post a stake against a verified listing, and a narrow, cryptographically-provable class of bad delivery can slash it on-chain without human arbitration. It is scoped tightly on purpose — Section 7 states plainly what it does not do, and that section should be read as load-bearing, not as a caveats appendix. Every decision below is final for v1; where an alternative was seriously considered and rejected, that reasoning is kept in the text rather than discarded, so a future reader can see why, not just what.

---

## 1. The Problem

x402 as it exists in this repo is a pay-then-fetch protocol: the buyer's payment settles on-chain, and only after settlement does the buyer make the HTTP request that's supposed to return the paid-for resource. Look at the actual code path — `src/server.ts`'s `/settle` route and `src/bazaar.ts`'s `onAfterSettle` hook fire only once `result.success` is true, and by that point the sponsor's signature has already authorized a real, final state transition on Soroban. There is no on-chain acknowledgment step between "payment settled" and "buyer got what they paid for," because those are two entirely different systems: one is on-chain ledger state, the other is an HTTP GET against whatever server the seller happens to be running. That asymmetry is the whole vulnerability. If a seller takes payment and returns nothing, garbage, or a 500, the transaction that already moved money is completely indifferent to that outcome — the facilitator has no idea it happened, the chain has no idea it happened, and the only party who witnessed it has, today, no mechanism to do anything about it.

This isn't a bug so much as the flip side of the exact feature that makes x402 attractive. Most conventional payment rails (cards, PayPal-style processors) have a review or dispute window before funds truly finalize; x402 deliberately has none, because instant finality with no processor and no chargeback risk is the pitch. So any fix has to preserve instant settlement — reintroducing an escrow delay would give up the thing that makes this worth using over a card — while still creating a recourse path that activates strictly *after* the fact.

On why a straight port of whatever x402r does on EVM/Solana doesn't just work here: this design does not claim knowledge of x402r's internal mechanism. No verified, sourced documentation of it was available, and asserting specifics about how it works without having read its code would be exactly the kind of unverified claim this document exists to avoid. What can be reasoned about with confidence is the actual difference in available building blocks. EVM chains have a mature ecosystem of general-purpose optimistic-dispute and attestation infrastructure — UMA's Optimistic Oracle, Chainlink, various EigenLayer-based attestation services — that a bond-and-slash system can compose with instead of building from scratch. Solana has a smaller but real equivalent (Pyth, Switchboard, and a fair number of agent-commerce-specific primitives, given how much of the "agentic payments" narrative has concentrated there). Soroban doesn't have an equivalent off-the-shelf oracle/attestation layer yet. It has the cryptographic primitives a bond contract actually needs — Ed25519 and secp256r1 signature verification as host functions, ledger-timestamp access for windows, the SEP-41 token semantics this facilitator already leans on for USDC — but there's no mature "someone already solved generic off-chain-fact-attestation" layer sitting underneath. That means this design cannot assume the hard part is solved and only Soroban bindings are needed; it has to decide, itself, what primitive lets a claim be checked by a contract without a human in the loop. **This design is built Stellar-native from first principles**, treating x402r's existence on other chains as proof the problem is solvable, not as a spec to replicate.

Why this matters specifically for agentic payments, more than it would for a human buying from a human-run storefront: a scammed human can complain, leave a review, charge back a card, or just stop using that seller — all of that is "human arbitration" in the loose sense, dispute resolution mediated by someone exercising judgment about what happened. An autonomous agent paying through the Bazaar discovery flow (`src/mcp.ts`, the `withBazaar` client) has none of that. It settled based on a machine-readable `accepts`/schema description, it's going to parse whatever comes back with code rather than judgment, and if what comes back is wrong there's no person in the loop to notice, complain, or decide whether the wrongness was fraud or a bug. Worse, at agent scale, one bad actor doesn't need to fool one human once — it gets listed in the catalog and drains a slow trickle of value from every agent that discovers it via search, with nobody noticing the pattern the way a human community eventually would through reviews or word of mouth. That's the actual stakes: the Bazaar's existing trust machinery — TOFU binding, Layer 2 ownership verification, the `trust` block's `ownershipState` — proves *who owns* a listing, but says nothing about whether that owner will *deliver*, and agentic buyers have no fallback when it turns out they won't.

**Locked decision — proof model.** "Provable" cannot mean "the chain directly observed a failure," because the chain never witnesses HTTP delivery. It has to mean "the claimant submitted evidence a contract can check without judgment." This design uses a **cryptographic receipt/commitment scheme**: the seller's response is accompanied by a signed receipt tied to the specific payment, and bad delivery is provable exactly when no valid receipt surfaces within a window, or the one that does is malformed or forged. A second option was considered and set aside for v1: a facilitator-attested oracle, extending the existing post-settlement probe pattern in `src/bazaar.ts`/`src/ownership.ts` to independently check delivery and post a trusted attestation. It would cover more failure modes (timeouts, malformed responses) at lower build cost, but it makes the facilitator's attestation key a single point of failure for the entire system — compromise it and slashes can be forged or blocked outright. The entire value of a bond system is that a buyer doesn't have to trust the seller *or* a third party for recourse; an oracle-backed version is really "the facilitator promises to make you whole" wearing an on-chain costume. The receipt scheme is strictly narrower in what it can catch (Section 3 states the boundary precisely, and Section 7 restates it as the single most important thing a reader should take away), but it doesn't trade away trustlessness to get there. The oracle option remains a plausible, explicitly-disclosed *supplement* to add later, not a v1 component.

**Locked decision — bonding is additive, never substitutive.** A listing must already be `ownershipState: verified` (Layer-2-proven, per `src/catalog.ts`) before a bond can attach to it. Ownership answers "is this really who they claim to be"; the bond answers "will they actually deliver" — two orthogonal questions, kept orthogonal in both the design and the data model (see Section 4). Letting a large bond substitute for ownership proof was considered and rejected: it would let a well-funded squatter buy their way past exactly the hijack and squat protections (`G-1`/`G-2`/TOFU) this catalog already spent real effort closing.

## 2. How the Bond Works

A bond is a stake tied to one specific listing, not a seller's identity in general — a reading taken directly from the requirement that a seller "posts a bond when they register a verified listing," and one that fits the additive model from Section 1: ownership verification already answers the identity question at the level of a single canonical resource key (`BazaarCatalog.canonicalResourceKey`, `src/catalog.ts:736-743`), and per-listing bonding keeps the stake scoped to that same unit. If a seller runs fifty listings, they post fifty bonds — more capital required, but a bad delivery on listing A can only ever slash the stake backing listing A, never bleed into money also backing forty-nine unrelated, perfectly good listings.

Posting a bond happens once a listing reaches `ownershipState: verified` — the seller sends a token transfer into the bond contract referencing the canonical resource key, and from that point the catalog's `trust` block reflects the bond's existence and size (Section 4).

**Locked decision — bond sizing is seller-chosen, displayed as a signal.** No protocol-enforced relationship between bond size and listing price exists beyond a dust-preventing floor; the bond amount is simply exposed in the catalog so buyers and agents can weigh a bond-to-price ratio themselves. Two alternatives were considered. A flat protocol-enforced minimum is simplest but economically weak — a listing charging $1000/call could post the same trivial bond as one charging a cent. A size proportional to declared price (bond ≥ N × the listing's settlement price) is harm-proportional by construction, but commits to a specific multiplier before any operating data exists to justify one, and forces a re-validation question every time a seller edits their price. Seller-chosen sizing is the lowest-effort option and the right amount of engineering for a first version of a brand-new primitive: the size-to-price relationship can graduate to an enforced rule later, once real slash claims show what the multiplier should have been, without migrating anything already bonded. The cost of this choice is real and is stated without softening in Section 5 and Section 7: a thin bond has no structural defense against a seller who simply outlasts it.

**Locked decision — bonds are posted in the same asset the listing charges in.** A USDC-priced listing bonds in that same USDC SAC. This needs no conversion and no price oracle anywhere in the contract; a slash pays the claimant back in exactly the denomination they lost. A single canonical bonding asset (e.g. always XLM) was considered for cross-catalog comparability, but it would require a price oracle at slash time to know what a claimant's actual loss is worth — reintroducing exactly the kind of new trusted input Section 1 was built to avoid, purely for the sake of a UI convenience.

**Locked decision — custody is a single shared contract, not one per seller.** One contract holds every bonded listing's tokens, with per-listing accounting in its own storage keyed by canonical resource key. A bug in this model is a systemic risk to every bonded listing at once — but that's the same risk profile as the USDC SAC itself, which this system already depends on, not a new category of exposure. The alternative, a dedicated contract instance per seller, would give stronger blast-radius isolation, but every registration becomes a real on-chain deployment, and a bug found after the fact means migrating every live instance individually rather than upgrading one contract — the opposite of the centralized-fix instinct this repo already shows elsewhere (the additive migration pattern in `src/store.ts:256-269` is the same instinct applied to the database).

**Locked decision — unlock timing tracks only the latest settlement's window.** The contract tracks one timestamp per listing: `lastClaimWindowEnd = lastSettlementAt + disputeWindowSeconds`. Withdrawal is permitted only once `now >= lastClaimWindowEnd` **and** no dispute is currently open. This is not a simplicity-for-safety trade-off — because claim windows are fixed-length and settlements happen in chain order, settlement N's window has always already closed by the time settlement N+1 lands, so tracking only the most recent one is exactly as protective as a full per-settlement ledger would be, for a fraction of the state. A per-settlement rolling ledger, tracking every settlement's own unlock timestamp independently, was considered and is strictly more complex with no corresponding protective benefit given fixed-length windows — one of the few places in this design where the simpler option isn't a trade-off at all.

This same mechanism is what prevents a seller from withdrawing a bond ahead of a slash: an open dispute keeps the withdrawal gate closed regardless of how much wall-clock time has passed, so a seller cannot pull funds out mid-adjudication no matter how long resolution takes. The mechanics are checked in detail, including the specific question of whether a race exists between "window closes" and "withdrawal executes," in Section 5.

## 3. How a Slash Works

Locking in the cryptographic-receipt model forces an honest reckoning with what it can and cannot prove. The seller's server, on responding to the buyer's HTTP request, produces a signed receipt: a signature over `(paymentId, responseHash, timestamp)`, using a key registered specifically for this purpose.

**Locked decision — the seller signs with a dedicated delivery key, not the funds-custody key.** The `payTo` key is exactly the kind of secret that shouldn't sit hot on an internet-facing server answering every request — this repo's own posture toward `SPONSOR_SECRET_KEY` (env-var only, never logged, dashboard-only in `render.yaml:65`) is the same instinct applied to a different key, and a seller's delivery-signing key deserves the same treatment for the same reason, just with lower stakes: compromising it lets someone forge delivery claims, not steal money directly.

What a contract can actually verify from a receipt is narrow and worth stating precisely: **whether a validly-signed receipt exists for a given `paymentId` within the claim window**, and **whether one that exists is well-formed** — right key, right format. What it fundamentally cannot verify is whether the content the receipt commits to is what the buyer actually received, because the only account of that off-chain HTTP exchange comes from one of two parties who each have an incentive to lie about it. A dishonest seller could serve different content than they hashed and signed; a dishonest buyer could equally claim they received something other than what actually arrived. Neither claim is checkable against anything the chain witnessed.

**Locked decision — this design accepts the resulting scope boundary explicitly.** What's provable, under this model, is **non-delivery** (no valid receipt ever surfaced) and **malformed or forged commitments** — never "the receipt was valid but described the wrong content." Verifying a content-mismatch claim would require trusting one party's account of an event the chain never witnessed — exactly the human-judgment-shaped gap this whole design exists to avoid needing. The alternative to accepting this boundary is reopening Section 1's proof-model decision toward a facilitator-attested oracle that can independently observe content, at the cost of the pure trustlessness already chosen. This document does not reopen that trade; Section 7 restates the boundary in the sharpest terms this document uses anywhere, because it is the single fact a reader most needs to walk away with.

**Locked decision — the dispute flow is optimistic, not proactive.** The seller does not post a receipt on-chain for every delivery — that would mean every settlement, even a one-cent quote, costs a second on-chain write, actively hostile to the microtransaction economics that make x402 worth using at all. Instead, the receipt is handed to the buyer off-chain, the same way `PAYMENT-REQUIRED` challenges already flow in this system's Layer-2 verification (`src/ownership.ts`). If the buyer is satisfied, nothing further ever touches the chain — the common case costs nothing extra. Only if the buyer got nothing, or got something without a valid receipt, do they file a dispute on-chain, referencing `paymentId`. Filing opens a short response window during which the seller can defeat the dispute by producing the same receipt on-chain — a pure signature-and-hash check the contract performs itself. If the window closes with nothing produced, the slash executes automatically via a permissionless finalize call, since Soroban contracts cannot self-trigger on elapsed time. A mandatory on-chain receipt for every delivery was the alternative, and was rejected specifically because it roughly doubles the transaction overhead of every settlement, including the cheap ones this protocol exists to make viable.

**Locked decision — only the verified payer of a settlement may file a dispute, with no counter-stake required.** Standing is limited to the party who might actually have been harmed. A counter-stake requirement was considered as an anti-spam measure but rejected: since a bogus claim is already cheap and mechanical for a seller to dismiss with a real receipt, a counter-stake mostly makes an honest buyer pay twice — once to buy, once to complain — for a threat that's already cheap to neutralize. Opening dispute-filing to any party, not just the recorded payer, was also rejected as a pure harassment vector with no offsetting benefit. This requires the bond contract to know `(paymentId → payer, seller, resourceKey, amount)` at settlement time, since a Soroban contract cannot discover this after the fact by inspecting arbitrary past transactions — the facilitator's settlement flow must register this explicitly (Section 6).

**Locked decision — the response window is a fixed protocol-wide constant, not seller-configurable.** A seller allowed to self-configure their own response window would have a standing incentive to set it as long as the protocol permits, purely to delay a legitimate slash — closing off that lever was judged more valuable than the flexibility a configurable window would offer sellers with unusual operational realities, a benefit that's speculative until real sellers demonstrate a need for it.

## 4. How It Integrates With the Bazaar

The additive model from Section 1 has a direct consequence for the data model: `ownershipState` does not change. It stays the exact three-value enum it is today — `unverified` / `proven-unconfirmed` / `verified` — because it answers one question, ownership, and the enum is load-bearing elsewhere (`clampByOwner()` in `src/trust.ts:387-390` clamps every per-accepts verdict by owner-verification status). A fourth value, something like `"verified-bonded"`, was considered as a single field a caller could key filtering on, but rejected: it would touch every existing consumer of the enum and re-couple two concepts this design deliberately keeps orthogonal, for a convenience the new fields below already provide another way.

**Locked decision — bond data lives in new, additive fields inside the existing `trust` block.** The catalog's `trust` block gains bond amount, bond asset, whether the bond is currently withdrawable, and a count of currently open disputes. Raw numbers are exposed, not a coarse tier or a flat boolean, because Section 2's seller-chosen sizing model only works if buyers and agents can actually compute their own bond-to-price ratio against the listing's own `accepts[].amount` — a summary judgment would throw away exactly the information that decision depends on being visible. The distinction between "has a bond" and "currently has *locked* funds protecting a prospective payment" matters and is represented explicitly: once a bond's dispute window has closed with nothing pending, the seller could withdraw at any moment even without having done so yet, and a buyer paying right now needs to know that, not just that a bond once existed.

**Locked decision — an open, unresolved dispute is shown as data, never used to suppress or demote a listing.** The open-dispute count appears directly in the trust block so an agent can see it before paying, without a second query — but the listing itself is not excluded from `verified_only`-style filters or demoted in ranking merely because a dispute is open. Suppression-on-filing was considered and rejected specifically because Section 3 deliberately made filing free on the premise that bogus claims are cheap for sellers to dismiss and low-consequence to receive; if merely filing a dispute already hurt a seller's visibility, that premise would break, and filing itself — not winning — would become the attack, precisely the griefing vector examined in Section 5.

**Locked decision — bonding gets its own filter, `bonded_only`, separate from `verified_only`.** The existing `verified_only` filter keeps its exact current meaning and behavior — no silent change for anything already depending on it, including the MCP discovery tools in `src/mcp.ts`. A caller wanting both guarantees combines `verified_only=true&bonded_only=true` explicitly. Redefining `verified_only` to also require a bond was considered and rejected as an undocumented breaking change to something already shipped — exactly the class of regression this repo's own audit discipline exists to catch, not introduce.

## 5. Attack Surfaces

### False slash claims

Filing a dispute costs nothing, and this design stands by that. Walking through what a griefer actually gets for the trouble: they file against an honest seller who really did deliver. The seller has a valid signed receipt already in hand — produced the moment the response was served, not scrambled together after the fact. Defeating the dispute costs one on-chain transaction: submit the receipt, the contract checks the signature, the dispute closes. A single false claim costs the griefer nothing and buys them almost nothing — a few hours of the response window elapsing, no reputational damage (Section 4 decided open disputes are visible but not suppressing), and the bond is never touched, because a defeated dispute never reaches the slash step.

The real exposure is volume, not any single claim. A griefer who has, or controls, genuine settlement history against a target seller has standing to dispute every one of those real payments. A hundred trivial payments become a hundred filed disputes, and the seller has to post a hundred receipts. Each is individually cheap, but it's a sustained operational tax — and if the seller's receipt-posting process has any failure mode at all, one of those hundred might land as a real slash not because delivery failed, but because the claim wasn't cleared in time under load a griefer manufactured on purpose. The actual vector is not "prove a false claim," which this design already makes close to impossible — it's "manufacture enough claim volume that a seller's response infrastructure, not their delivery, becomes the thing under attack."

**Locked decision — per-payer dispute filing is rate-limited.** This reuses the shape of the facilitator's existing rolling-window spend limiter (`src/policy.ts`), but the enforcement point is the bond contract itself, not this facilitator's HTTP layer — a dispute can be filed by direct on-chain contract call, bypassing any facilitator API entirely, so the limiter has to live where filing actually happens. No rate limit, relying solely on the response window being short enough to blunt volume, was considered and rejected: it collapses the defense into a single parameter instead of two independent layers, making the whole mitigation depend on tuning one number correctly. A rate limit plus a counter-stake specifically for filers with no prior settlement history against a seller was also considered and rejected as reopening a question already closed above for a marginal gain the rate limit alone already covers.

**Locked decision — the withdrawal window stays fixed regardless of dispute volume; it does not dynamically extend under detected attack.** Dynamic extension was considered as an adaptive defense, but rejected: it adds real complexity (what counts as "high" volume, who computes it, whether an attacker could deliberately trigger extension to lock up a bond longer than the base window intends) for protection the rate limit above already provides more directly. The response window itself is sized for normal operations, on the assumption that the rate limit is doing the volume-suppression work — that split of responsibility, rate limit stops volume and window handles normal-case timing, is cleaner than asking one parameter to do both jobs.

### The bond withdrawal race

Walking the mechanics rather than asserting soundness: a listing's bond contract storage tracks, at minimum, `lastSettlementAt`, `disputeWindowSeconds`, and the set of currently-open dispute IDs. `lastClaimWindowEnd` is not separately stored — it's computed on read as `lastSettlementAt + disputeWindowSeconds`, so there is no second piece of state that could fall out of sync with the first. A withdrawal call evaluates, atomically, within a single contract invocation: is `now >= lastClaimWindowEnd`, and is the open-dispute set empty. Both checks run against the ledger state at the moment the withdrawal transaction executes, and Soroban contract execution is atomic within a single invocation — nothing can interleave a `fileDispute` call inside an in-progress `withdraw` call the way a race would require in a multi-threaded system. There is no gap between "check passed" and "funds move" for a new dispute to slip into.

The place a race could theoretically live is not the check itself but ledger-level transaction ordering: if a seller's withdrawal transaction genuinely lands in an earlier ledger than a buyer's dispute, and at that moment the gate's conditions were legitimately true, the withdrawal legitimately succeeds — the gate cannot reach back and retroactively block something that completed before anything was filed. That's not a soundness gap; it's the gate correctly enforcing "you can't withdraw while something is pending," which by construction cannot protect against a dispute that hadn't been filed yet. The real question this raises is not "is there a race in the check" — there isn't — but whether the window is long enough that a buyer who wants to dispute realistically gets the chance to file before a seller's incentive to withdraw the instant the window closes becomes a live, exploitable strategy. That is a sizing question the fixed, protocol-wide window (Section 3) is meant to answer, not a defect in the gate.

### Minimal bond, ignore disputes

This has a real threshold, not just a vibe. A seller posts the dust floor and plans to let it get slashed occasionally rather than deliver reliably. Whether that's profitable depends on the relationship between the bond size `B`, the per-settlement price `p`, and the settlement volume `N`. Over `N` settlements the seller collects `N × p`, and loses at most `B` total — once a bond is fully slashed there is nothing left to slash on subsequent disputes. So the take is roughly `N × p − B`, profitable whenever `N × p > B`, which for a dust-floor bond and any nontrivial volume is almost always true almost immediately — a bond worth one settlement gets outrun by the second payment, disputed or not.

**Named, accepted v1 gap, stated without softening:** a seller-chosen bond with no protocol-enforced floor relative to price has no structural defense against this strategy at all. It relies entirely on the display mechanism in Section 4 actually working — an agent that reads the bond amount against the listing's price before paying, and discounts trust for a thin ratio. If the consumer side of the ecosystem doesn't reliably do that, especially early, before "check the ratio" is a norm anyone's built tooling around, the protocol offers zero backstop. Adding a protocol-enforced minimum bond-to-price ratio now was considered and rejected for the same reason Section 2's sizing decision rejected it originally: there is no operating data yet to justify a specific multiplier, and picking one wrong either underprotects buyers or prices honest sellers out. This gap is carried forward into Section 7 rather than minimized.

### Collusion

This is the strongest result in this section, and the proof model earns it rather than assuming it. A colluding seller-payer pair attempting to drain a *competitor's* bond would need to route a real settlement through the competitor's canonical resource key — an actual payment, at the competitor's declared price, landing in the competitor's own settlement flow against their own real bond — and then have the colluding "payer" file a false dispute. Here is where the model holds: the competitor, an honest and entirely uninvolved third party, served the request normally and produced a real, validly signed receipt exactly as they always do, sitting exactly where it always sits. When the colluding payer files, the competitor's receipt-posting behavior doesn't know or care who's disputing — it responds the same way it responds to any dispute, by posting the same real receipt, and the false claim is defeated by the same mechanical signature check as any other. **The cryptographic-receipt model, specifically because it doesn't depend on the disputing party's honesty at all, is exactly what makes this attack not work**: an attacker can manufacture a false claim, but cannot manufacture a missing receipt for a delivery that genuinely happened, because the receipt's existence is a fact about what the honest, uncoordinated seller actually did, not about what the attacker asserts.

This "route a payment, file a false claim" version of collusion reduces to the same volume-griefing surface analyzed above — costly to defend against at scale, but not capable of landing a slash against an honest, responsive seller. The genuinely dangerous collusion shape is not a novel mechanism at all; it's the volume-griefing attack pursued with intent against a specific target, and it shares the same mitigation (the per-payer rate limit) rather than needing one of its own.

**New operator runbook incident, added to the docs plan below per explicit follow-up:** *"A settlement succeeded but its bond registration never landed."* Symptom: a buyer's dispute for a real, valid payment fails or is rejected because the bond contract has no record of that `paymentId`. Confirm: cross-reference the facilitator's own settlement log against the bond contract's registration state for that `paymentId` — a mismatch confirms the gap, not a false dispute. Steps: since `register_settlement` is gated to the facilitator's own authorized identity, a manual backfill through that same authorized path is the remediation. Verify: confirm the backfilled registration exists and that a synthetic dispute against that `paymentId` now resolves correctly. This closes the specific failure mode where synchronous registration (chosen above) still leaves a gap between "payment succeeded" and "registration confirmed," and ensures that gap has a documented recovery path before this ships rather than being discovered live by an affected seller.

### Explicitly not in scope: `vellar-explorer`

The explorer's entire data pipeline is architecturally independent of the facilitator's catalog — its own indexer polling Soroban `getEvents` directly, its own classification heuristic that deliberately does not trust facilitator-reported fields. It has no concept of "listing," "ownershipState," or "resourceKey" anywhere in its schema today, and its own Sellers page already carries a disabled "Bazaar" button captioned "No Bazaar integration built yet." Surfacing bond data there would mean a second independent contract-watcher, a new per-listing storage shape, a new API surface, and a new page — comparable in size to building support for the `upto` scheme was. Correctly a follow-on, not v1 scope.

## 7. What This Does Not Solve

A bond posted against a listing is not insurance against being disappointed. It is a narrow, specific, cryptographically-checkable guarantee against exactly two things: a seller who took payment and produced nothing, and a seller who produced a forged or malformed commitment. That's the whole guarantee. Everything below is real, and none of it is an edge case someone is unlikely to hit — these are the ordinary ways this system will not help, stated as they'd actually be encountered, not as engineering footnotes.

**If you get a response, you get nothing.** This is the biggest limitation and it needs to be said without hedging: if a seller answers a request — with the wrong data, a low-quality result, a partial answer, something that doesn't match what the listing advertised, anything at all that isn't literally silence or a broken signature — the bond cannot be slashed, full stop. The signed receipt a seller produces proves they committed to *something* at the moment of delivery. It does not, and structurally cannot, prove that something was correct, because verifying correctness would require the contract to trust one party's account of an event it never witnessed — exactly the human-judgment problem this whole system exists to avoid needing. A buyer scammed by a plausible-looking wrong answer has exactly as much recourse today as they would with no bond system at all: none.

**Most sellers ever transacted with will never have posted a bond.** Bonding is opt-in, layered on top of ownership verification a seller may or may not have completed either. There is nothing in this design that requires it, nudges the catalog toward requiring it, or defaults new listings into it. A buyer or an agent doing discovery should assume, as the default case, that the listing in front of them carries no bond at all, and act accordingly — the system will not warn them differently unless they specifically check.

**A bond protects only up to whatever the seller chose to post, and that number can be trivially small relative to what it's protecting.** There is no protocol-enforced relationship between bond size and price. A seller can post the minimum dust floor and nothing more. The arithmetic in Section 5 is not hypothetical: once a seller's cumulative revenue from a listing exceeds its bond size, continuing to take payments and simply eating the occasional slash becomes economically rational, not deterred, for a bad actor. This system does not stop that. It relies entirely on a buyer or their agent actually comparing the posted bond against the listing's price before deciding how much to trust it, with no guarantee that check happens.

**Nothing here verifies who a seller is, or whether they're trustworthy in any general sense.** Ownership verification proves someone controls a specific URL. A bond proves someone locked up a specific amount of a specific token. Neither is identity verification, a background check, or a reputation signal in the way a marketplace review system is. A well-resourced, patient bad actor can pass Layer 2 verification and post a real bond and still operate entirely within the narrow slice of behavior this system cannot see.

**The anti-griefing defenses reduce the cost of bad behavior; they do not make it free of cost to a seller, or impossible.** A rate limit on dispute filing stops unlimited free shots at forcing a response, but a motivated, patient attacker with real settlement history against a target can still impose a genuine, sustained operational tax — and if that seller's own receipt-posting process ever slips under that load, a slash can land even though nothing was actually undelivered. This is a known, accepted trade-off, not a solved problem.

**This only works if everyone involved actually built the integration.** A seller fully compliant with plain x402 but never wired up delivery-receipt signing gets none of this protection, bond or no bond — there is no retroactive coverage for a listing that didn't implement the mechanism. A buyer whose client never checks for a receipt or never files a dispute within the window gets no protection either, regardless of what the seller did. This is a right that has to be actively exercised on both sides, within a real, finite window, not automatic insurance that activates on its own.

**If someone reads only this section:** treat a bond as proof a seller has skin in the game against vanishing outright, and nothing more. It says nothing about quality, nothing about honesty beyond that one narrow axis, and nothing about a seller never transacted with before beyond what they chose to stake. Anyone evaluating this system — a reviewer, a buyer, an integrator — should walk away understanding it closes one specific, real gap in x402's settle-before-delivery design, and leaves every other way a transaction can go wrong exactly as open as it is today.
